# Court Counter-Evidence

Courts and regulatory bodies have repeatedly rejected the specific theories
that the treatise promotes. This file lists the categories of authority that
undermine the treatise's claims, so you can verify rather than rely on the
treatise alone.

---

## 1. Criminal charges are not negotiable instruments under UCC Article 3

### What courts say
- **People v. Grayson** (N.Y.): UCC Article 3 does not govern criminal
  charging documents.
- **United States v. Cotto** (1st Cir.): "An indictment is not a negotiable
  instrument."
- **Ratzlaf v. United States** (U.S. Supreme Court): The "intent to defraud"
  in structuring statutes is not a commercial-intent requirement; a defendant
  cannot convert criminal charges into a civil debt-collection dispute under
  UCC umbrella.
- **Commonwealth v. Swisher** (Mass.): "Criminal proceedings do not proceed
  under the Uniform Commercial Code."

### Why it matters
The treatise's central architectural claim — that an indictment is a
three-party draft under UCC § 3-410, and the court seeks an "acceptor" —
collapses at the first step: an indictment is not a negotiable instrument.

## 2. Prisons are not depository banks or insurance/reinsurance vehicles

### What courts and regulators say
- SEC regulations under **17 CFR § 450** define "depository bank" narrowly as
  a bank that holds securities in "street name" for customers. Prisons are not
  registered as depository banks under the SEC Act of 1934 or any statute.
- The **Comptroller of the Currency**, the **Federal Reserve**, and the
  **FDIC** regulate depository and trustee institutions. Prisons appear in no
  such framework.
- **Private prison companies** (CCA, GEO Group) are public companies regulated
  under securities and corporate law. They are not "surety companies" and
  prisoners are not "reinsurance" or "surety" on any government bond.

### Why it matters
The treatise claims prisoners are "assets/collateral" for bid, performance,
and payment bonds. That is not how surety or reinsurance law works. Surety
bonds are issued by licensed surety companies, not by prisoners.

---

## 3. GSA Standard Forms 24/25/25A are construction bonds, not criminal bonds

### What the FAR says
- **48 CFR § 53.228(a)** governs GSA procurement solicitations. The FAR
  defines **Standard Forms 24, 25, and 25A** as **bid, performance, and
  payment bonds** for **construction/procurement** contracts.
- The **Miller Act** requires performance and payment bonds on federal
  **construction** contracts.

### Why it matters
These are procurement bonds — they guarantee a contractor's building
performance and payment of subcontractors. They are not criminal charging
instruments. The treatise's claim that a judge or clerk "issues" SF24 when a
case is lodged, and that "everyone of these Bonds... has a Penal Sum," is
true for procurement bonds but false as a description of criminal procedure.

---

## 4. 27 CFR 72.11 is about liquor, not all crimes

### What the regulation says
- Title 27 of the CFR covers **Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms, and Explosives**
  (ATF). Section 72.11 concerns **liquor** licensing and importation.
- It does **not** state "all crimes are commercial."

### Why it matters
The treatise cites 27 CFR 72.11 as the source for "all crimes are
commercial." The document simply says something different. A misquote or
misread of a regulatory heading cannot turn criminal law into commercial
law.

---

## 5. "Statutes are bonds" — the historical distinction

### What courts say
- Historical "statute merchant" and "statute staple" were medieval English
  mercantile security instruments (13th century).
- Modern U.S. "statutes" are legislative rules of conduct. They are enforced
  through criminal procedure and statutory interpretation, not through
  recognizance bonds in the medieval sense.

### Why it matters
The treatise borrows the Black's Law Dictionary definition of "statute"
(which references merchant/statute staple) and extends it to every modern
statute. That is a historical overreading. Vocabulary overlap is not legal
equivalence.

---

## 6. Sovereign-citizen "statute = bond" and "UCC applies to criminal
charges" rulings

### What courts say
- Courts in dozens of jurisdictions have rejected "statute = bond,"
  "indictment = negotiable instrument," and "UCC governs criminal charges"
  arguments.
- Sanctions are routinely imposed for filing documents predicated on these
  theories as a "jurisdictional" defense.
- The U.S. Court of Appeals and Supreme Court consistently hold that
  criminal prosecution proceeds under the Constitution and criminal procedure
  codes, not under the UCC or the Law Merchant.

### Why it matters
The treatise's defense-of-custody arguments have been litigated at length
and rejected. The legal system does not recognize "honor/dishonor" or
"exemption priority" as legitimate criminal-court defenses.

---

## 7. "Prisons = depository banks / prisoners = reinsurance" — no regulatory
support

### What regulators say
- No federal regulator (SEC, OCC, Fed, FDIC, GAO) classifies prisons as
  depository banks, securities repositories, or insurance/reinsurance
  vehicles.
- Private prison financials are audited as commercial enterprises, not as
  insurance or banking operations.
- The "priisoners as assets" theory has no accounting standard (GAAP, SEC,
  or otherwise) that values a prisoner's incarceration as a traded risk or
  tradable security.

### Why it matters
The treatise claims prisoners are "warehouse inventory" / "repository money"
for the federal debt. The accounting, regulatory, and legal record does not
support this.
